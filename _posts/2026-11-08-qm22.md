When we first looked at the [Hadamard gate](../qm13/) there arose three matrices representing directions in physical space. They were introduced a little abruptly like background characters in someone else's story, and they deserve some more spotlight. Here they are again:

$$
Z =
\begin{bmatrix} 
1 & 0 \\ 
0 & -1 
\end{bmatrix} 
\quad
X =
\begin{bmatrix} 
0 & 1 \\ 
1 & 0 
\end{bmatrix}
\quad
Y =
\begin{bmatrix} 
0 & -i \\ 
i & 0 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Geometrically they are all simply reflections. This is most obvious in $Z$, because they're all written in the $Z$ basis. $Z$ just flips the sign of the second coordinate (the one that scales $\ket{1}$) and has no effect on the first coordinate (the one that scales $\ket{0}$). Or to put it another way, a vector of the form $c\ket{0}$, where $c$ is any complex scalar, is entirely unaffected:

$$
Z c\ket{0} = c\ket{0}
$$

whereas a vector of the form $c\ket{1}$ experiences a sign-flip:

$$
Z c\ket{1} = -c\ket{1}
$$

So these, being only scaled by $Z$, are the eigenvectors of $Z$, whereas any other vectors will not only be scaled, they will be re-aligned. In general any state vector $\ket{\psi}$ can be written:

$$
\ket{\psi} = a\ket{0} + b\ket{1}
$$

The probability of measuring state $\ket{0}$ is $aa^* = |a|^2$, and for $\ket{1}$ it's $bb^* = |b|^2$. But the act of multiplying (say) $a$ by its own complex conjugate has the effect of erasing any overall complex phase, because:

$$
\left( e^{i\theta} \right)^* = e^{-i\theta}
$$

and:

$$
e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta} = e^{i\theta-i\theta} = e^0 = 1
$$

This means that although $Z$ flips the sign of $\ket{1}$, this does not constitute an effect on anything physically significant about the state, because a sign flip is just a phase factor.

The other matrices represent the two orthogonal directions to $Z$. $X$ swaps the coordinates between $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$, performing a reflection around the $45^\circ$ line lying between them. This is the line that $\ket{+}$ sits on (if you've read the previous entries, you'll be familiar with the diagram by now.) So $Z$ has no effect at all on $\ket{+}$, meaning that here $\ket{+}$ plays the same role that $\ket{0}$ played under $Z$. Likewise, a vector aligned with $\ket{-}$ would flip to point the opposite way. So if we were to rewrite all these matrices in the $X$ basis, then the $X$ matrix would have that same simple $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ form that we've given to the $Z$ matrix.

$Y$ is apparently the most exciting, in that it seems to combine both ingredients seen so far: a sign difference between the coordinates (like $Z$) and a swapping between the coordinates (off-diagonal entries only, like $X$), but to these it adds an overall factor of $i$. Again we can see how it affects its eigenvectors, which are given the mnemonic labels $\ket{+i}$ and $\ket{-i}$ to remind us of the coordinate on $\ket{1}$:

$$
\ket{+i} = \frac{\ket{0} + i\ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}
\quad \quad
\ket{-i} = \frac{\ket{0} - i\ket{1}}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

Visualising this geometrically at first *seems* next to impossible (the $\ket{1}$ component has been scaled *by a complex rotation* that nevertheless doesn't change its alignment), but based on the pattern so far, we'd expect $Y$ to have no effect on $\ket{+i}$ (we can ignore the overall $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ because it appears on both sides of the equation):

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 
0 & -i \\ 
i & 0 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 
1 \\ 
i 
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 
1 \\ 
i 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Check! Whereas it should perform an overall $-1$ scale on $\ket{-i}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 
0 & -i \\ 
i & 0 
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 
1 \\
-i 
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 
-1 \\ 
i 
\end{bmatrix}
= 
-
\begin{bmatrix} 
1 \\ 
i
\end{bmatrix}
$$

All is as expected. In fact we can visualise it geometrically if we just relabel the axes on our usual diagram (come on, it's been a while). When we use the $Z$-basis unit vectors $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ to express the eigenvectors of $X$, we quietly ignored $Y$, leaving it off the diagram. Well, now we can switch allegiance:

<script type="text/tikz" alt="The Z-basis with a twist">

    \draw (0,0) circle (1);
    \draw[dashed,->] (0,0) -- (1,0) node[anchor=west] {$\ket{0}$};
    \draw[dashed,->] (0,0) -- (0,1) node[anchor=south] {$i\ket{1}$};
    \draw[dashed,->] (0,0) -- (45:1) node[anchor=south west] {$\ket{+i}$};
    \draw[dashed,->] (0,0) -- (-45:1) node[anchor=north west] {$\ket{-i}$};
    \draw[dashed,->] (0,0) -- (-225:1) node[anchor=south east] {$-\ket{-i}$};

  \end{tikzpicture}
</script>

What is this voodoo?! Actually we're still technically in the $Z$-basis here, because our vertical axis $i\ket{1}$ is just as much of a valid eigenvector of $Z$ as is $\ket{1}$. Our diagrams have to be real subspaces of $\mathbb{C}^2$. We normally draw the real subspace spanned by $\lbrace \ket{0}, \ket{1} \rbrace$, now we're using the real subspace spanned by $\lbrace \ket{0}, i\ket{1} \rbrace$.

In Bloch sphere terms, it gets even simpler. First, think of the usual $\lbrace \ket{0}, \ket{1} \rbrace$ diagram. It is easily visualised as a circular slice through the Bloch sphere (with one important caveat). If you follow a point moving around that circle, it visits $\ket{0}$, $\ket{+}$, $\ket{1}$, $\ket{-}$ in that order. Or to use their physical-space names instead of computing names, $\ket{u}$ (up), $\ket{f}$ (front), $\ket{d}$ (down), $\ket{b}$ (back):

There is no way to access $\ket{l}$ (left) $\ket{r}$ (right) from the provided ingredients *if* you are restricted to using only real coordinates to scale them. It's the $X$-$Z$ great circle, no $Y$ ingredients are supplied, so the rest of the sphere is out of bounds. The caveat I mentioned is that the circle on the flat state space diagram is not precisely the same circle as this slice through the Bloch sphere, because $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ are *orthogonal* in the state space, whereas in the corresponding Block sphere great circle they are *antipodes* (on opposite sides). The angle separating any two states is doubled on the Bloch sphere.

Now with $\lbrace \ket{0}, i\ket{1} \rbrace$ as the axes of our state space diagram, we can access a great circle that visits $\ket{0}$, $\ket{+i}$, $i\ket{1} \propto \ket{1}$, $\ket{-i}$, or $\ket{u}$, $\ket{r}$, $\ket{d}$, $\ket{l}$, never $\ket{f}$ or $\ket{b}$. It's $Y$-$Z$ only, and has no $X$ ingredients for us to scale with our strictly real coordinates:

Note how the state diagram's $i\ket{1}$ only differs by a phase factor ($-1$) from $\ket{1}$, so these refer to the same point on the Bloch sphere.

We've still only scratched the surface of this topic. For example, the matrices are reflections in 2-dimensional complex state space. What are they in the Bloch sphere, that is, in real 3-dimensional physical space?
