The reflections , , were figured out by Pauli, who started with some basic constraints imposed by what was already known in 1927 (a pretty important year in QM.) First, it was known since 1922’s Stern-Gerlach experiment that if you put a beam of silver atoms through a linear magnetic gradient, they behave as if every atom is a little bar magnet (a unit of magnetic moment) that is aligned exactly with the gradient, one way or the other. Half of them go up, half go down, the beam is split so makes two bright dots on the screen. This was surprising because you’d assume from classical intuition that they would fan out, each atom having a random orientation relative to the gradient.

Second, Dirac had already explained that in QM, if a quantum system has a measurable property with two possible outcomes, the vector state space for that system is two dimensional. Putting these first two concepts together, the state space describing the orientation of a particle’s magnetic moment must be two dimensional.

Third, by feeding the “up” output particle beam from the above experiment into exactly the same kind of apparatus, but with the magnetic gradient rotated , the beam splits again, left/right. This makes sense if you think of each particle’s magnetic moment as a dot on a sphere, and each of these stages acting as a filter that only allows through particles whose dot is in one of the two hemispheres, divided by an equator that depends on the alignment of the magnetic gradient. If the particles’s dots are randomly distributed over the sphere of possible spatial directions, only half of them have their dot in the upper hemisphere, and then of that beam, only half qualify as right hemisphere, and so on. The second stage’s rightward beam must, you’d naively think, therefore contain only particles whose dot is in the upper-right quarter (they’ve made it through both filters.)

And so a third stage aligned to split up/down again should only produce a single up beam. But it produces two equal beams, up and down. From this we are forced to conclude that each stage, when treated as a filter, has the effect of snapping every particle into alignment with its field gradient, one way or the other, and that therefore the second stage wipes out the alignments produced by the first stage. Also there seems to be a random choice made for each particle as to which way to snap into alignment.

Again, Dirac had already established the rules of QM here: the two dimensional (complex) state space for particle alignment has to represent mutually exclusive measurement outcomes, such as up/down, with orthogonal vectors, and other (incompatible) observable outcomes, such as left/right, must be expressible as a sum of up/down, and vice versa. Also observables correspond to self-adjoint operators (Hermitian matrices). With these rules in place, and the results of Stern-Gerlach, Pauli found that there was exactly one way to arrange the state vectors for three orthogonal spatial directions, and the operators for the observables corresponding to those directions are the three reflections around , and , which are , and respectively.

Now, the first thing to notice about Pauli’s three matrices is that they are traceless. This means that the sum of their eigenvalues is zero, as they are QM operators, thus self-adjoint, thus normal, which is to say amenable to being diagonalised, in which form they are all just . They can be linearly combined to represent any alignment in space, to make Hamiltonians (energy operators) that steer the evolution of a particle’s spin orientation.

But any such combination will also have zero trace (the trace is a linear operator, so the trace of a sum of matrices is the sum of their individual traces). Therefore we can’t make any old matrix with these three ingredients, only those that possess this particular quality of balanced eigenvalues on either side of zero.
