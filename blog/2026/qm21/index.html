Such a two-slot machine can be given one input, say for the first slot, which clogs up that slot, resulting in what is in effect a one-slot machine. Generalising further, the scalar that falls out after both inputs are provided is, in a sense, a zero-slot machine!

The symmetry of the notation where a bra acts on a ket to produce a scalar, , can just as well be read as the ket acting on the bra; when implemented as a calculation in terms of coordinates, it has the same machinery as the familiar dot product: coordinate-wise multiplication to produce terms in a sum. So you can completely flip the picture around and visualise a ket as a one-slot machine that takes a bra as its input, and the tensor product of two kets as a two-slot machine, and so on.

To supply a density operator to the slots is to ask a question. If we supply , that’s a simple projection operator representing a specific state, and the question is: “What’s the probability that the system is in this state?” and is the answer.

The deeper point is that this two-slot case, while a valid way to describe any complex system, is a simplification if we are interested in the subsystems that comprise that complex system. Each subsystem can be thought of as having its own bra-ket pair of slots, and we can eliminate one subsystem by tying its slots together.

These two viewpoints are perfectly compatible because the tensor product allows us to connect the state spaces of any group of subsystems together into one state space. We can group them into a hierarchy, deal with them individually or all at once. If our system contains qubits, we can divide them into three registers of , and qubits each, each register exposing two slots. It’s vital to remember that they might not be three independent machines: their behaviour maybe correlated by entanglement.
