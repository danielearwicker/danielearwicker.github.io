Quantumania 20 - Disentanglement

Created on November 02, 2026

2026   ·   physics   qm

QM says the state of an isolated physical system is a well-defined thing, which I’ve characterised as sharp and definite, rather than blurry and uncertain, and which we’ve found two ways to represent: a vector in the state space, or a projection operator on that state space:

We can use the measurement operator for some measurement outcome (which can be as simple as the projection operator for the corresponding post-measurement state) to find the probability of an outcome, but that’s all. This implies some intrinsic uncertainty in how the universe works, sometimes called ontological probability, because the probability is aspect of the world, rather than just being due to our own lack of knowledge, which would be epistemic probability. If we attempt to measure some property, and the present state is not one of the post-measurement states associated with that property, we will nevertheless get a definite answer corresponding to one of the property’s post-measurement states, and it seems that nature chooses that answer at random. This is, in a sense, our own fault for asking the wrong question.

But the moment we measure an isolated system, it ceases to be isolated. Indeed, you could say that it never was isolated, as we were there the whole time too, along with the measuring equipment, all just subsystems of some larger system. This led us to consider composite state spaces accessed via the tensor product, which in turn led to the awkward realisation that sometimes the definite state of the composite system did not contain definite states for its subsystems. Only if the composite system is in a product state, meaning the tensor product of the states of the subsystems, can we regard the subsystem as having definite states of their own. It is possible for you to know exactly the state this whole system was prepared in, and yet still not know what state the individual qubits are in, not because of epistemic uncertainty, but because the two qubits don’t have a state of their own. Here’s where the idea of a mixed state really becomes useful. We introduced it by considering boring old epistemic uncertainty, a coin flip whose result Bob doesn’t know. But we can use exactly the same approach to represent something like the actual state of one of the individual qubits, or the closest thing it has to a state. Its state is intrinsically mixed, in reality itself, not just in our minds because we lack information. The mixed description is all the information there is in the world about that one qubit.
