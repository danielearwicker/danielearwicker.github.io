What we’re going to do with our solid United Nations-slash-Flerfer map is use it to represent both operations-on-things and things-being-operated-on. To do this, we’ll stick strictly to unit quaternions, which have modulus and are a closed set under multiplication: if you multiply two unit quaternions, the result is a unit quaternion. The things-being-operated-on are points in space, and the operations-on-things are rotations, and they are both simply points in our solid ball map. In fact, they are the only kind of quaternions in our map.

The unit quaternions describe all the possible pure rotations (with no scaling) that can be performed on a 3D space, just as unit complex numbers describe all pure rotations (with no scaling) on the complex plane. I can think of two possible nagging objections you might feel about this.

So now we need to figure out the same thing for the quaternion space: what is the action of a unit quaternion on all the points in that space? So that we can see the results on our solid model, which only contains unit quaternions, we’ll restrict our space of points to be operated on to the unit quaternions with zero scalar part, which sit on the 2-sphere boundary between the inner core and the outer shell. That way we can visualise points moving around on our map.

This looks exactly like ordinary complex rotation, interchanging between the real number line and whichever imaginary component we chose to multiply by, but the effect of it on our ball-map is to drag anything not on the circle around in a kind of weird cycle that includes an apparently discontinuous flip from positive to negative . Really of course this discontinuity is a side-effect of our projection to 3D. The unit quaternions with positive and negative are really two halves of the hypersphere surface.

In your mind, set the and coordinates of a test quaternion to zero, while allowing and (the real, scalar coordinate) to cycle around the surface. The test quaternion travels through the North Pole () smoothly and without incident. Why can’t it do the same at the South Pole? It can, and does! It passes through () just as smoothly. It’s just that in our projection to 3D, we exploded the South Pole into the outermost sphere, the 3D ice wall of our 3D United Nations map, where all points have coordinates . So anything that passes through the South Pole appears, on our projection, to discontinuously teleport. When has a tiny positive value, it’s right next to the ice wall on one side of the outer sphere, and when it passes through to become slightly negative, it’s then just inside the ice wall on the opposite side.

Despite this, our projection still makes sense because the kinds of rotation we want are interchanges between imaginary pairs like , which look like well-behaved rotations in the projection space. But an interchange between an imaginary component and the real number line totally destroys this neat picture.

The one saving grace is that, because we’re always acting with unit quaternions on test subjects that are also unit quaternions, the product is always another unit quaternion, so at least they don’t disappear from the map entirely. Our map has no place for non-unit quaternions. It’s just a unit-only slice of the space of all possible quaternions, as we threw away non-unit quaternions to achieve a 3D projection. Any operation on a test quaternion that moves it away from the surface of the unit hypersphere will make it vanish entirely from our map, “into the fourth dimension”! A somewhat science-fictiony phrase that isn’t particularly helpful, because if something exists in a 4D space then obviously it has always been somewhere in the fourth dimension, just as it has always been somewhere in the other three. But it would no longer be sitting in the one 3D slice visible in our map.

So the action of a quaternion with an imaginary component, such as , is in effect two simultaneous rotations:

  • the one we want: the quaternion defines an axis around which the other two imaginary components rotate, so the plane tumbles nicely around it.
  • the one we don’t want: complex real-imaginary interchange that our projection converts into cyclical discontinuous teleportation through the ice wall.
